# The Treaty That Failed Twice — presenter script

7 slides · ~5 minutes · slide 5 is the only one that animates (4 clicks)

**Rule: never read the slide.** The slide holds the four words, you hold the sentence.

---

### Slide 1 — "The treaty that failed twice" · 25 sec
Plastic production is set to nearly triple by 2060 — 460 million tonnes in 2019,
a projected 1,231. In 2022, 175 countries agreed to write a treaty to stop that.
They have now failed twice.

What I want to show you is not the failure. It's what the failure did to the
people fighting it.

### Slide 2 — "One resolution, five sessions, no treaty" · 35 sec
In March 2022 the UN agreed to negotiate a binding treaty covering the full life
cycle of plastic. Production, use, disposal.

Five rounds of talks, finishing December 2024. That deadline came and went.
So did the next one.

### Slide 3 — "Both rounds died on the same split" · 60 sec
Both rounds collapsed on the same split.

On one side, around seventy countries wanting binding limits on how much plastic
gets made. On the other, oil-producing states who wanted a treaty about bins and
recycling only.

Two hundred and thirty-four fossil-fuel lobbyists registered at Geneva.
**[pause two seconds]** That was more people than the entire European Union sent.

Panama's delegate said: we will not betray future generations. The talks ended
with no deal.

### Slide 4 — "Is plastic a waste problem or a [production] problem?" · 45 sec
Everything comes down to one question. Is plastic a waste problem, or a
production problem?

If it's waste, you fix it with recycling and cleanups. If it's production, you
have to tell oil companies to make less. That's why this is hard.

And the science moved the argument. Researchers found microplastics in human
artery plaque, and in human brains. Plastic stopped being about turtles and
started being about us.

*(Those square brackets are the motif of this deck — every one is something
countries still argue about.)*

### Slide 5 — "Losing twice radicalised the movement" · 85 sec · YOUR KEY SLIDE
**Slow down. One click, one sentence, then stop.**

So here's what changed. Four shifts.

**[click 1]** One — the movement stopped trusting consensus. When any one country
can veto everything, campaigners now demand majority voting instead.

**[click 2]** Two — it stopped trusting company promises. Weeks after Busan
failed, Coca-Cola quietly weakened its recycled plastic targets.

**[click 3]** Three — it moved upstream. From cleaning beaches to capping
production.

**[click 4]** Four — it went to court. California is suing ExxonMobil for telling
the public that plastic was recyclable when it knew most of it was not.

The movement did not get quieter after losing. It got harder to deal with.

### Slide 6 — "Alive, but shrinking" · 35 sec
February this year, countries elected a new chair, Julio Cordano of Chile. He
restarted talks in Nairobi in July.

But he kept production off the agenda — and the draft he published leaves
production out entirely. France called it disappointing.

Formal negotiations now happen in March 2027. So the process survived. The
ambition is shrinking.

### Slide 7 — "Is a [weak] treaty better than no treaty?" · 25 sec
The movement's own answer is no. Its slogan now is that no treaty beats a bad
treaty.

But that's a gamble — because while they hold out, production keeps rising.

So I'll leave you with the question they're arguing over right now. Is a weak
treaty better than no treaty at all?

## Delivery notes

- **Total ~5:10 spoken.** Rehearse three times with a timer; it always runs
  longer live. If you need to cut, trim slide 4's science paragraph.
- **Pause before the point, not after.** The two-second silence after "234" is
  the single most important beat in the talk.
- **Slide 5 is the graded slide.** Rehearse it most. One click per sentence — do
  not click ahead of yourself.
- Every slide's script is also in the PowerPoint speaker notes, so presenter
  view will show it.
